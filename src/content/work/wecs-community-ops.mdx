## The real deliverable

Student clubs run on goodwill and improvisation, which works beautifully right
up until the person holding everything in their head graduates. As Community
Events Director for WECS, my actual job isn't any single event. It's building
an operations system that makes good events repeatable by whoever comes next.

<Sidenote n={1}>The test I hold myself to: could someone run our next event
from the documentation alone? If the answer is no, the documentation is
the task.</Sidenote>

## A finding I didn't expect

One craft event per semester, always on a Wednesday, always capped by budget
&mdash; and they still pull noticeably harder than most other WECS events.

<div class="wecs-attendance">
  <div class="wa-bar"><div class="wa-fill"></div><span class="wa-n">~20</span><span class="wa-l">Other events</span></div>
  <div class="wa-bar"><div class="wa-fill"></div><span class="wa-n">30&ndash;40</span><span class="wa-l">My craft events</span></div>
</div>

I don't run these through partnerships with other clubs or industry sponsors.
The whole loop is mine: pick the craft, buy the supplies, build the playlist,
and stand at the tables helping people finish theirs. Spots are capped by
budget, not demand, which is exactly why the survey and the applied-learnings
note matter: every semester's craft has to earn its slot against a real
constraint, not just against how fun it sounded in a meeting.

## Why this one matters

This is the least visual project on this site and the most representative of
the work I want to do: quiet systems, honest measures, and documentation good
enough to hand to a stranger. It is also the kind of work whose value grows
with scale, which is exactly why I intend to keep doing it.
